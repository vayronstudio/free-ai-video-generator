Imagine sending a message from New York to London. In less than a second, it can cross an entire ocean. Your video loads, your bank transfers money, and millions of people communicate across continents. But here's something strange. Almost none of that information travels the way you probably imagine. It doesn't fly through space. It travels through something hidden beneath thousands of meters of seawater.

Most people imagine the internet as an invisible network floating above our heads, powered by satellites and wireless signals. But the real foundation of global connectivity is much closer to the ocean floor. More than 95 percent of international data and voice transfers travel through submarine cables. These cables connect continents like invisible bridges. And without them, the modern world would look very different.

At first glance, the technology seems almost ridiculous. The infrastructure responsible for connecting billions of people can be surprisingly thin. In deep ocean areas, many cables are roughly the width of a garden hose. Inside them are extremely thin glass fibers, some no thicker than a human hair. But these tiny strands carry enormous amounts of information using something remarkably simple: light.

IMAGE: Extreme macro visualization of a transparent glass fiber. Bright pulses of light travel through it at incredible speed. The camera enters the fiber and follows the light through its microscopic structure.

Every photograph, email, video, and website is converted into digital information. That information is represented by patterns of light traveling through glass fibers. Lasers send pulses through the cable, and equipment at the other end interprets them back into data. Instead of electricity traveling through a traditional copper wire, information moves through light. And this is what makes modern submarine networks so powerful.

IMAGE: A historical recreation of the 1850s. Engineers stand beside a massive wooden cable drum on a nineteenth-century ship. A thick telegraph cable disappears into the gray ocean while sailors struggle with ropes and machinery.

But this technology didn't begin with smartphones or streaming services. The idea of connecting continents beneath the ocean is much older. In the nineteenth century, engineers attempted something that seemed almost impossible: sending electrical messages across the Atlantic. Early submarine telegraph cables were fragile, difficult to manufacture, and incredibly expensive. Yet the ambition was enormous. People wanted to communicate across oceans without waiting for ships to deliver messages.

---

IMAGE: A dramatic historical recreation of the 1866 transatlantic cable expedition. A giant steamship crosses a stormy Atlantic Ocean while releasing cable into the water. Show a close-up of the cable disappearing beneath the waves.

After several failures and enormous engineering challenges, a successful transatlantic telegraph cable entered service in 1866. Suddenly, messages could cross the Atlantic in minutes instead of taking days or weeks by ship. It was a revolutionary moment. But the cable could only transmit simple telegraph signals. Nobody could have imagined the amount of information that future generations would send through the same oceans.

---

IMAGE: A cinematic transition through time. Old telegraph equipment transforms into twentieth-century telephone switches, followed by early computers and finally a modern fiber-optic cable glowing beneath the sea.

Over the following century, submarine communications evolved. Telegraph systems gave way to telephone cables, which could carry multiple conversations at once. Then came a breakthrough that changed everything. In 1988, the first transoceanic fiber-optic cable system, TAT-8, connected the United States, Britain, and France. Instead of depending on traditional electrical signals, it used light traveling through optical fibers. The modern internet age was beginning.

But how do you place something so delicate inside an environment that can crush ordinary equipment? The answer is careful engineering. A submarine cable is not simply a bundle of glass thrown into the ocean. Its construction depends on its location. Near shore, where fishing equipment and ship anchors create serious risks, cables receive additional layers of protection. Farther into the deep ocean, they can be much lighter because human activity is less common.

---

IMAGE: A vast underwater landscape showing a cable passing through a continental shelf. A specialized cable-laying ship appears above the water. An underwater plow carefully creates a narrow trench and places the cable beneath the seabed.

The journey begins long before the cable touches water. Engineers survey the seabed, studying its depth, geology, slopes, and potential hazards. They search for a route that avoids dangerous terrain and heavily used fishing or anchoring areas. Near coastlines, specialized equipment can bury the cable beneath the seabed. In deeper water, the cable is often laid directly on the ocean floor. Every kilometer is planned because repairing a mistake thousands of meters underwater is anything but simple.

---

IMAGE: A massive cable-laying vessel on a calm ocean, viewed from above. Huge cable drums dominate the deck. Workers monitor equipment while the ship slowly releases a continuous cable into the sea. Show the cable descending through the water column.

Now comes the extraordinary part. Specialized ships carry enormous amounts of cable and slowly release it as they follow a carefully planned route. The ship must control its speed, the cable tension, and the amount of cable being released. Too little cable can leave it stretched dangerously tight. Too much can create unwanted loops. The operation may take weeks or even months, depending on the distance and complexity of the route.

And eventually, the cable reaches a world most humans will never see. Thousands of meters below the surface, sunlight has disappeared completely. The pressure is immense, temperatures are low, and the landscape can remain almost untouched for centuries. Yet the cable continues working. It rests quietly on the seabed, carrying information between continents every second. But there is one problem. Light cannot travel forever without losing strength.

IMAGE: A detailed scientific visualization of a submarine fiber-optic cable with a series of cylindrical optical repeaters positioned along it. The camera moves toward one repeater and reveals its internal optical components.

Over enormous distances, the optical signal gradually weakens. To solve this problem, engineers install devices called repeaters along the cable. These underwater devices restore the strength of the optical signals so information can continue traveling across the ocean. They are positioned at regular intervals, often tens of kilometers apart. And unlike ordinary equipment, these devices must keep working reliably for years without anyone opening them for maintenance.

IMAGE: A cinematic close-up of an optical repeater glowing in the darkness. A transparent cutaway reveals optical amplifiers and electronic components. A subtle animated electrical current travels along the cable from a coastal station.

But here's another surprising detail. These cables don't just carry information. They also need electrical power. A dedicated conductor supplies direct current from equipment at the landing stations to the underwater repeaters. This means that a cable connecting distant continents can carry tiny pulses of light for communication while simultaneously receiving electrical power to keep the system operating. It is a remarkable combination of optical science and electrical engineering.

IMAGE: A coastal cable landing station at sunset. A cable emerges from a protected underground duct and enters a secure industrial building. Inside, rows of optical transmission equipment glow with status lights as engineers monitor the network.

Eventually, the cable reaches land. This is called a landing point. But the ocean cable doesn't simply connect directly to your phone. It connects to specialized facilities where optical signals are managed and linked to terrestrial fiber networks. From there, information travels through data centers, internet exchanges, telecommunications infrastructure, and local providers. The ocean is only one part of an enormous system. Yet it is the part that connects the continents.

And the scale of this network is extraordinary. More than 600 submarine cable systems are tracked around the world in 2026, including active and planned systems. Together, they form an infrastructure stretching across more than a million kilometers of ocean. Some cables connect neighboring countries. Others cross entire oceans. Some are so long that their routes stretch for tens of thousands of kilometers. And every new connection creates another path through which the world's information can travel.

So what actually happens when you press play on a video? Your device sends a request through your local network. That request moves through your internet provider and across interconnected networks until it reaches the server hosting the video. If that server is on another continent, submarine cables may carry the information across the ocean. The video then travels back through the network in packets, arriving so quickly that the entire journey feels almost instantaneous.

And entertainment is only the beginning. International banking, stock markets, cloud computing, business communication, scientific research, and government services all depend on this infrastructure. Even a small delay can matter in some applications. Light travels extremely fast, but it still takes time to cross an ocean. And because fiber-optic cables provide high capacity and low latency compared with many satellite connections, they remain essential to global communication.

But the system has a weakness. These cables may be hidden beneath the ocean, but they are not invulnerable. Fishing equipment and ship anchors can damage them. Underwater earthquakes, landslides, and other natural hazards can also cause failures. In fact, the International Telecommunication Union reports roughly 150 to 200 submarine cable faults globally each year. Most are accidental. And when one breaks, the consequences can reach far beyond the ocean.

In March 2024, multiple submarine cable disruptions off the west coast of Africa caused major internet connectivity problems across several countries. Services were affected in places including Côte d'Ivoire, Liberia, Ghana, and others. Some networks struggled, and traffic had to find alternative routes. It was a powerful reminder that a problem on the ocean floor can affect millions of people who may live hundreds of kilometers away from the damaged cable.

So how do engineers repair something that has broken thousands of meters beneath the sea? They cannot simply send a diver down with a toolbox. Instead, a specialized repair vessel travels to the fault location. Engineers use information from the cable system to identify the problem area, then deploy underwater equipment to locate and recover the damaged cable. In deep water, this operation requires highly specialized ships, machinery, and experienced crews.

Once the damaged section is recovered, technicians inspect the cable and remove the broken portion. They prepare the remaining ends, connect replacement cable, and carefully splice the delicate optical fibers together. The repaired section is tested before being lowered back into the ocean. In some situations, buoys temporarily support the cable during the operation. What looks like a simple line on a map can require a complicated marine engineering mission to restore.

Fortunately, the global internet is not normally dependent on one single cable. Many regions have multiple submarine connections, and networks can reroute traffic when one path fails. But this protection has limits. If several important cables are damaged at the same time, or if a country depends on only a small number of connections, the disruption can become much more serious. The internet is resilient, but resilience depends on having enough alternative routes.

And this creates another question. Who controls these cables? The answer is more complicated than you might expect. Some are owned by telecommunications companies, while others belong to groups of investors or major technology companies. Companies such as Google, Meta, Microsoft, and Amazon have invested in submarine cable infrastructure. The reason is simple: controlling more of the infrastructure helps them build the capacity needed for their enormous global services.

Cloud services, artificial intelligence, international research, and high-definition media all require enormous communication capacity. New cables are being planned and installed to meet this demand.
